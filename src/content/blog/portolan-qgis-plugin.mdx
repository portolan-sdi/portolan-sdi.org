One of the first things that Chris Holmes and I talked about at the beginning of this year when kicking around the idea of Portolan was a QGIS plugin that would let users browse and download data from any published Portolan dataset. Our goal was to make it really simple for QGIS users to tap into what we consider "key" open datasets around the world -- like ESA WorldCover or Overture Maps or GHSL population data -- without needing to track down and wrangle different files scattered across different portals, each requiring different tools. This is now possible: last week, we released a [Portolan registry plugin in QGIS](https://plugins.qgis.org/plugins/portolan_registry_qgis/), making any dataset in the Portolan registry accessible in QGIS, too.

## What's in the plugin?

Currently, the plugin allows users to browse all catalogs in the Portolan registry, explore the data, and import or download it with a click. You can filter by map extent or a polygon boundary to import only the data you need, for both vector and raster data.

Users interested in more information on the dataset can see relevant documentation, such as the dataset extent, README, and license, or click out to open the full data documentation in the Portolan browser.

Because Portolan catalogs are built on strict, consistent standards for cloud-optimized files and catalog structure, the plugin requires no complicated backend infrastructure -- just GDAL and DuckDB. Users might not know (or care) that the underlying data are cloud-optimized and organized in STAC catalogs; all that matters for them is that it's now easy to access datasets like JRC's [global flood hazard](https://browser.portolan-sdi.org/#/external/data.source.coop/nlebovits/jrc-glofas/catalog.json), or [Overture building footprints](https://browser.portolan-sdi.org/#/external/nlebovits.github.io/overture-portolan/catalog.json), or [GHSL population](https://browser.portolan-sdi.org/#/external/data.source.coop/nlebovits/ghsl/catalog.json), or [Fields of the World](https://browser.portolan-sdi.org/#/external/data.source.coop/ftw/global-data-2e/catalog.json), all in a single, simple UI. This is particularly important because previously, most of these datasets were scattered across different portals, in different formats, meaning that an analyst's time was primarily about finding and wrangling data, not using it. Now, with all of these datasets available through a consistent UI, analysts can spend less time searching and more time actually working with the data.

## Why QGIS?

QGIS is one of the most democratic geospatial tools available. It's free and open source, with [millions of users](https://www.cell.com/patterns/fulltext/S2666-3899%2825%2900113-8), many of whom are in the Global South. During my Fulbright research in Argentina, pretty much everyone I met who did geospatial -- whether at universities, in local governments, volunteer firefighters, etc. -- was using QGIS, whether due to the cost of an Esri license, the technical barriers to using R or Python, or just the momentum stemming from the large QGIS user community. Because of this, making data available in QGIS means reaching a whole new audience: namely, users who would never otherwise be able to access these data through, say, `pystac-client` or the Copernicus API. This should be particularly compelling to institutional data publishers like JRC, the World Bank, or World Resources Institute, who often spend a lot of time and effort and money producing datasets that then sit in storage somewhere and don't have nearly the reach they could simply because it's hard for less technical users to access them.

With this QGIS plugin, any catalog [added to the Portolan registry](/registry) shows up automatically in the plugin, which means that institutional publishers can make their data available without spinning up custom infrastructure. And, because the plugin is meant to host a wide range of possible data -- while also being easily searchable -- people will hopefully start their searches here, much like in the case of ArcGIS' open data portal, meaning that your data are more likely to be found here. (As a bonus: if your data are hosted on [Source Cooperative](https://source.coop), you get an immediate sense of usage thanks to the built-in analytics.) Eventually, we are hoping that uptake of this plugin will actually drive new traffic to all of the catalogs in the Portolan registry -- people who otherwise would never have known about or used the data in question -- demonstrating that making data more accessible also makes it more used.

## What's next?

In future releases, I'll improve the UI and performance, and try to add features as they're requested. (See the [GitHub repo](https://github.com/portolan-sdi/portolan-registry-qgis/issues) for a list of currently planned features.) I also plan to add multilingual support soon (initially for English, Spanish, French, and Arabic). Youssef Harby of CARTO plans to work on an ArcGIS version of the plugin, and Qiusheng Wu has already built a version for GeoLibre. While managing Portolan catalogs is explicitly out of scope in *this* plugin due to the complexity involved, we do hope to develop a plugin for this in the future.
