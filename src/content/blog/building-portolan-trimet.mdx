So this is the first post in what is hopefully a collaborative series for the
Portolan blog posts. The idea is to show off some of the cool results of
Portolan catalogs that have joined [the registry](/registry), and to share how
things actually got built. In these early days of Portolan it may also likely
include enhancements to the ecosystem or even the spec that came from working
with the data. My hope is that Portolan grows to be more than just a set of
tools and a spec, but it's a big community of people working to make geospatial
information more accessible. Together we can experiment with new ways of not
just organizing data, but also how it's presented - in apps, in chat
interfaces, in maps, and in hybrids of all three.

I'll kick things off with a Portolan mirror of Portland TriMet's open data.
TriMet is the transit agency for the Portland OR region, and they have a really
special place in my heart as they were the very first customer of 'GeoServer
Enterprise Edition'. It was the first product I ever launched, and having such a
respected government agency as a first customer was huge. A month or so ago I
was on the way to visit my family in Oregon, and had set up a visit to see
[Bibiana McHugh](https://bibianamchugh.com/), who lead that first purchase, and
also pioneered [GTFS](https://gtfs.org/) and
[OpenTripPlanner](https://www.opentripplanner.org/).

They've always been leaders in open data, and I'd long been a fan of their nice
simple page that makes it easy to just 'get the data':

To me it's way better than some managed 'portal' with all kinds of (often
unmanaged) tags and categories, that costs significant money to run and
maintain. But I was curious if we could 'upgrade' it with Portolan - retaining
the 'simple' spirit of it and keeping it all as just files that anyone can
download, but making it more interactive and more accessible for AI agents. So
5 minutes before I boarded my flight I put this prompt into claude:

```
can you make a complete portolan catalog from the 8 datasets at https://developer.trimet.org/gis/ - use the latest portolan spec from ~/repos/portolan-spec and add 3-5 styles for each entity, based on analysis of the data, plus great agents.md files as described in the spec best practices. Do include links to each of the original shapefile / kml /metadata, and then adapt to pmtiles / geoparquet and mine the metadata. Do research anything else you can find about these datasets, to include more context. And be sure to include trimet logos in the catalog.
```

Thankfully I was on Delta, with free wifi, so I was able to iterate away with
claude on the plane, and by the time we landed a couple hours later I had a
fully working catalog along with a custom
[Portolan Browser](https://browser.portolan-sdi.org/#/) themed to match their
aesthetic. And the results were quite compelling - you can explore yourself at
https://cholmes.github.io/trimet-data-browser/.

## The Gory Details

So I was quite happy with the results. I'm going to now go deep into the
details, so if you don't actually care about how these catalogs get built then
feel free to jump past this section. For those who are really interested, you
can see my complete session with claude in
[this markdown file](https://gist.github.com/cholmes/0c572f0f21952498d24345a7a2e8adb6).
Though one thing to note is that this was using an early version of the
[Portolan Skills](https://github.com/portolan-sdi/portolan-skills/blob/main/skills/reading-portolan/SKILL.md),
and we've refined those based on learnings from this session, so I believe
trying this again would need less than a third as much prompting. Today I'd
likely just start with a simpler prompt to 'make a complete portolan catalog
from the 8 datasets at https://developer.trimet.org/gis/', as most of the
specifics I said are now incorporated into the skill.

### git-backed catalog

This was also one of the earliest 'git-backed catalogs', from before we added it
as [a best practice in the spec](https://github.com/portolan-sdi/portolan-spec/blob/v0.2.0/specs/best-practices/git-backed-catalogs.md).
Read the full best practice for all the details, but the idea is that you store
the 'non-data' (stac catalog.json, agents.md, readme.md, etc) in a git
repository, along with the set of scripts that formats your data and publishes
it. The data itself stays out of git as it bloats your repository, but all your
other changes get versioned. And the scripts should enable complete
reprocessing of the data if it gets messed up. This method has emerged as
essential when working a lot with AI agents, as you can be confident that if it
messes something up you can roll it back and not lose everything. You can see
this catalog at https://github.com/cholmes/portolan-catalog-trimet. The cool
thing is that we added a way to
[link a catalog to its repository](https://github.com/portolan-sdi/portolan-spec/blob/v0.2.0/specs/best-practices/git-backed-catalogs.md#link-a-catalog-to-its-repository)
to enable 'issues' where people can report problems with the catalog, and 'pull
requests' where people and agents can directly propose improvements. We see a
future world where the agents reading catalogs can be checking for issues and
report them back or directly improve the catalog being queried.

We put together a
['template repository'](https://github.com/portolan-sdi/portolan-catalog-template)
that makes it easy to start a repo with all of these practices incorporated -
you can just clone it and adapt to your needs. The other key thing that the
template sets up is the continous integration (CI) that will run rashid and
stac tests on all edits, so you can be sure that all edits are actually still
valid. This also catches lots of errors, and is highly recommended.

### Custom-themed Browser

The other thing that was fun for this one was really tweaking the browser to
match TriMet's styling. I started with my
[Portolan Browser fork](https://github.com/portolan-sdi/portolan-browser/), but
it's just some customizations on top of STAC Browser, that
[Matthias Mohr](https://moregeo.it/team) has really evolved into a great
product. Way back in the prehistory of STAC Browser I was always excited to have
it be customized - that each org would run their own STAC Browser instead of
everyone just using the publicly hosted one. But it never happened much. I think
initially it wasn't that easy to do, but even with improvements that Matthias
made to make it easier we still didn't see a lot of custom ones. I believe AI
coding tools will change this, as it was so easy for me to customize. I just
pointed claude at the developers page, and also the TriMet home page, and it
ended up quite close. The two sites were a bit different so I leaned towards the
developers one, using its fonts and colors a bit more, as this felt like it
'fit' closer to that. But this was the first time I tried to really stretch the
styling, and it was cool to see how easy it would be for an organization to
customize the browser to completely match the look and feel of their other
properties.

### Custom-basemap styling

One last customization, that was enabled by TriMet being great open data
citizens, was to use the exact same basemaps in their applications. They publish
all the maptiler tiles and styles they use at (todo - find this location, and
screenshot it). So it was super easy to pull in their basemaps, instead of using
the generic basemaps of STAC Browser. This really makes it feel unique - I wish
more organizations would customize their map tiles. This is now super easy to do
with claude plus vector tiles, so I hope we start to see more of it.

Ok, pulling out of the gory details, I think there are three main things I want
to highlight. The first is how easy this is to do. I spent no more than 3 hours
on the entire project of building the catalog _and_ the custom data browser.
Most of it ran independently on the plane, and then I did some iterations on the
results. I'd guess that doing the same project with the progress we've made in
the last month would make it less than an hour of work to get to where I did.
Yes, you need Claude or another capable agent, and it's probably a few dollars
worth of tokens if you're paying API list rates. Of course the agent is most of
what makes this so easy, but it's also the simplicity of the output - all the
agent needs to do is format the files right, and then all the cool tooling 'just
works'.

The second is the cost. I ran some analysis of the cost to store the data and
serve it up. The cost to store 7.6 megabytes of data at S3 standard list prices
of $0.023 a month is $0.002 per year. Yes, you read that right. This is a
particularly small set of data, but even if you had 1000 times this amount of
data it would be $2.00 per year to store the data. The bigger potential cost is
'egress', the only other thing charged for storing data on the cloud. This is an
amount charged for the total bandwidth downloaded by users. For interactive map
views of this data the cost depends on the actual traffic, but a busy government
site of 20,000 map sessions (142 gigabytes transferred and 1.7 million requests)
a month would be $4.45 on AWS S3. But if you used a content distribution network
(AWS cloudfront or Cloudflare R2) then it'd be $0. If you went fully viral, like
on the front page of Reddit or a national news story, with 5 million people
showing up, then a month could be $3000 if it's on S3, but if it's on Cloudflare
R2 it'd be $150. With a traditional arcgis server / geoserver this would be at
least ten times as much, and more likely would just crash the site unless
advanced auto-scaling was already in place. We'll be working on a cost
calculator to let people play with their numbers and see what it would cost on
common cloud-providers, including sovereign ones like Hetzner or Leaseweb.

And then the third is that the data is also now completely 'AI-enabled'. I'm not
going to do a deep dive on this now, as on the Portolan Blog we'll dig into more
examples of what this means, but I'll give a brief overview. And in the future
I'll try to circle back on this data, as there's a couple other Portolan
catalogs I want to get up first to show how you can do really interesting
queries across TriMet, Portland, Oregon and national census data. But you can
see my early experiments - I prepped a couple datasets into a local Portolan
catalog and then was able to ask questions about shade, car ownership, transit
equity and more, and got insightful responses back.
